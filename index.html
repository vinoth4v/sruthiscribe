SruthiScribe tracks the pitch of your voice and decodes it through the grammar of your ragam — into svara notation with tala bars, sahitya, and a PDF you can practise from. Free, in your browser; nothing you record leaves your device.

How does it work?

It tracks the pitch of your voice in real time and decodes it against the grammar of your ragam — svarasthanas, gamaka, sruthi drift — into readable notation. Underneath is a community library of compositions across every melakarta and dozens of janya ragams, searchable and correctable by anyone who knows better.

An AI teacher (Claude, by Anthropic) reads the transcription with you: it names the ragam it hears, says plainly when a phrase is too unclear to call, and flags where the reading itself looks unreliable. Free, runs in your browser, and nothing you record leaves your device unless you share it.

How accurate is it?

Scored on 33 studio recordings from the Sanidha dataset (Georgia Tech), against the dataset's own pitch annotations. The same harness runs the engine this page ships, not a copy of it.

91.7%svaras named right, octave included
82.5%raw pitch accuracy
2.5%octave errors
3.2¢sruthi (Sa) error

What that means in practice: expect roughly one svara in ten to need correcting, which is why Fix mistakes exists. Known weak spots —

27%of sung frames it declines to name rather than guess
32%of silences still get called a note
15%ragam guessed right first try — so you name it
5gamaka classes, not the dasavidha taxonomy

It is measured on solo voice; accompaniment in the room degrades it. It measures pitch, it does not hear bhava, and it is not a substitute for a teacher.

Setup Change
01

Set the sruthi

Everything downstream is measured against Sa, so getting this right first matters more than any other setting.

  1. Pick your Sa below — a note, octave, and Hz reading (a tanpura app or your teacher's reference pitch works; if unsure, leave the default).
  2. Tap Play Sa drone — it plays a sustained Sa continuously, like a tanpura.
  3. Sing "Sa" along with it. If your voice sits right on the drone with no beating or wavering pitch gap, that's your Sa. Tap Stop when done.

02

Choose the ragam

The ragam does real work here: it decides whether the note 300 cents above Sa is called R3 or G2, and it tells the decoder which pitches to expect — which is most of the accuracy.

Talam: which of the seven suladi sapta talas, and which jathi sets the laghu length. Adi (chaturasra triputa, 8 beats) is by far the most common.

03

Bring the audio

One voice at a time reads best. A solo phrase or an alapana works well; a full kutcheri track with violin and mridangam needs the separation step on the server.

Notation

Tap any svara to correct it — pick the right svara, shift its octave, mark it as a transit tone, remove it, correct its held-beat commas, group it into a faster speed with the next svaras, or set it as beat 1 of an avartana to draw the tala bars. Corrections carry into Copy, JSON and the PDF.

Community kriti database

Your reading is matched against a shared database of notated compositions. Matches can suggest corrections; your fixes can be contributed back as a new version for everyone.

Only contribute notation you have the right to share: your own transcriptions of traditional material, or corrections to existing entries. Contributions are public and stored as immutable versions.
Tune the decoder

Raise resistance for slow, heavily ornamented singing; lower it for fast swara passages. Widen gamaka tolerance if a single oscillating svara is breaking into several notes. Two corrections run automatically: if your voice sits measurably off the chosen Sa, the decoder re-tunes its grid to your voice before naming svaras; and each ragam’s ārohaṇa/avarohaṇa direction rules steer the decoder away from svaras that don’t belong to the direction you’re moving in.
